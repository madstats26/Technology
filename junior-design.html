Design process

The steps of the design process, how to research, generate ideas, test and evaluate.

The design process

The design process is a way of solving problems step by step. It has seven stages, and it is iterative: you go back and improve as you learn.

1. Analyse the problem

  • Read the brief carefully and figure out exactly what it is asking you to do.
  • Underline the key words in the brief, then write them down and say what each one means for your design.
  • Write down any words you don't understand, then look them up or ask your teacher.
  • Describe who it is for and where it will be used.
  • From this, write a specification: a list of what your solution must do, e.g. "must be no taller than 300 mm", "must be safe for a child to use".

2. Research the area

  • Look at existing products and note their good and bad points.
  • Do primary research: ask the people who will use it (a short survey or interview), or measure the user and the space.
  • Research the mechanisms, electronics and materials you might use.
  • Record every source, including websites and AI tools.

3. Idea generation

  • Mind map: put the brief in the middle and branch out to the user, what it must do, mechanisms, electronics, materials and possible ideas.
  • Mood board: fill a page with colourful images linked to your theme.
  • Three initial design sketches: three different ideas, drawn freehand in 3D, each annotated and labelled with materials, how it moves, how it is controlled and sizes.

4. Finalise your design

  • Choose the best idea, or combine the best parts of a few.
  • Justify it using the key words from your brief and specification.
  • Explain why you chose your mechanism and your electrical/electronic circuit.
  • Draw the final design with sizes and materials, the circuit diagram and a parts list.

5. Model / prototype

  • A prototype is a first model. Use cheap materials such as card or foam, or a CAD model in Onshape or Tinkercad.
  • Test the mechanism, and simulate or breadboard the circuit.
  • Take photos and record what you found.

6. Make improvements

  • Use your testing to find what did not work or could be better.
  • Change one thing at a time, then test again. This is iteration.
  • Explain every change and why, with before and after photos.

7. Evaluate

  • Test your finished product against the brief and the specification.
  • Write three successes, three challenges and three skills and other learning experiences.
  • Say what you would improve next time.

Worked example

Brief: design a bedside lamp for a child's room, made from recycled materials.

1. Analyse: key words underlined: bedside, child, recycled. User: a young child. Needs: safe (low voltage, no hot parts), gentle light, easy to switch on. Specification: height under 300 mm; at least one recycled material; LED light with a switch; stable base.

2. Research: photos of three bedside lamps, a short survey of parents, LED and switch research, recycled materials available at home.

3. Idea generation: a mind map, a mood board of soft colours and night-light shapes, and three annotated 3D sketches: a jar lamp, a tin-can lamp and a card lantern.

4. Finalise: the jar lamp, because glass jars are easy to find, the base is stable and the LED stays cool. Final drawing with sizes, circuit diagram and parts list.

5. Prototype: a card model to check the size, and the LED circuit built in Tinkercad Circuits.

6. Improve: the lamp tipped when the switch was pressed, so the base was widened and weighted. Before and after photos recorded.

7. Evaluate: checked against each point in the specification, then three successes, three challenges and three skills learned.
