Design process

The steps of the design process, how to research, generate ideas, test and evaluate.

The design process

The design process is a way of solving problems step by step. It is iterative: you go back and improve as you learn. Your teacher may name the steps slightly differently.

StepAsk yourselfWhat to record
1. Identify the problemWhat is the problem? Who has it? What is the brief?The brief in your own words; the user and their needs
2. ResearchWhat already exists? What works, what does not?Photographs, notes and sources of existing products
3. SpecificationWhat must my solution do (size, materials, cost, safety)?A list of measurable requirements
4. Generate ideasHow many different ways could I solve this?Annotated sketches of several ideas
5. Choose and developWhich idea best meets the specification, and why?Reasons for your choice; a developed drawing
6. PlanWhat materials, tools and steps do I need, and in what order?A materials list and a plan or timeline
7. Make and testDoes it work? What goes wrong?Photographs of making; test results; changes
8. EvaluateHow well does it meet the brief and the specification?An honest evaluation and improvements

Brief and specification

The brief describes the problem. The specification lists what the solution must do: for example, "must be no taller than 300 mm", "must use a recycled material", "must be safe for a child to use".

Research methods

  • Look at existing products and note their good and bad points.
  • Ask the people who will use it (a short survey or interview).
  • Measure: sizes of the user, the space and similar objects.
  • Look up materials and how they behave.

Generating ideas

  • Brainstorm: write down as many ideas as you can without judging them.
  • Sketch several different ideas, and annotate them with materials, sizes and how they work.
  • Combine and modify ideas, or change one thing about an existing design.
  • Use a mood board for appearance and style.

Prototypes and testing

  • A prototype is a first model. Use cheap materials such as card or foam, or a CAD model.
  • Test it against the specification and with the user.
  • Record what you found and what you changed. This is iteration.

Evaluating

  • Compare your finished solution with the brief and the specification.
  • Include test results and feedback.
  • Say what worked, what did not, and what you would improve.

Worked example

Brief: design a bedside lamp for a child's room, made from recycled materials.

User: a young child. Needs: safe (low voltage, no hot parts), gentle light, easy to switch on. Specification: height under 300 mm; uses at least one recycled material; LED light with a switch; stable base. Ideas: three annotated sketches. Choice: the jar lamp, because glass jars are easy to find, the base is stable and the LED stays cool. Test: check the circuit works, the lamp does not tip, and the child can use the switch. Evaluate: compare with each point in the specification.

Keep your evidence in a learning log. See the Projects page.
