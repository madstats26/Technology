The course

Applied Technology develops your understanding of, and skills in, how technologies are applied and how they affect the world around you. It is studied over the three years of Junior Cycle, with a minimum of 200 hours.

1. Principles and practices

Designing and making. Using materials, tools and processes to create solutions that suit the end user, and choosing suitable ways to solve real problems.

2. Energy and control

How energy is changed or controlled so that devices work safely and efficiently, with attention to economical and sustainable use of energy and materials.

3. Technology and society

How technology and society affect each other, including the environmental impact of design choices, user needs and social issues.

Four skills you use in every strand

  • Analysis and problem solving
  • Design and innovation
  • Planning, managing and creating
  • Communicating

Classroom-Based Assessments (CBAs)

Your teacher judges these. They are not part of the 70/30 split. Each is reported on your Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement (JCPA) with one of four descriptors: Exceptional, Above expectations, In line with expectations or Yet to meet expectations.

CBA 1: Exploring the application of controlled systems in a local context (2nd year, term 2)

An investigation of a controlled system in your local setting (classroom, school or community). You either look at an existing system with a defined function, or propose a new one. You have a maximum of 3 weeks, with teacher support. The evidence is your individual learning log, which can be supplemented by models, artefacts or other evidence. You can collaborate, but your piece of work is individual.

Examples: an emergency stop system in a workshop, corridor lighting, an entry or exit system, a communications control system, a radio-controlled system, a motion-controlled system for inclusivity in school.

What your teacher looks at: research and analysis, function, evaluating, and communicating.

DescriptorIn short
ExceptionalCompares a range of sources and gives a comprehensive, detailed analysis. Investigates the system in high detail with excellent understanding of its function. Evaluates critically throughout and reaches accurate conclusions. Uses a highly effective medium.
Above expectationsEffective research with in-depth analysis. Investigates the system in detail with very good understanding of its function. A high-level evaluation with relevant, accurate conclusions.
In line with expectationsAppropriate research with some analysis. Some reference to the function. An appropriate evaluation with brief conclusions. Well presented.
Yet to meet expectationsResearch is ineffective and the analysis lacks depth. Little detail, little or no reference to the function, little or no conclusion. The format makes communication ineffective.
CBA 2: Student self-analysis and evaluation (3rd year, term 1)

You analyse and evaluate your own coursework and skills so far, identify your strengths and areas for improvement, and use this to plan your project. You have a maximum of 3 weeks, with teacher support, and you can present it in any suitable format.

Coursework elements you could look at: researching, analysing findings, presenting work, generating ideas, sketching, bench skills, machine skills and final finishing.

What your teacher looks at: self-analysis of coursework elements, making judgements, and communicating.

DescriptorIn short
ExceptionalIdentifies a broad range of coursework elements with insightful, detailed observations and a comprehensive self-analysis. Critical judgements on strengths and improvements, showing exceptional awareness of how they will inform future work.
Above expectationsA broad range of elements with detailed observations and some in-depth self-analysis. Detailed judgements showing very high awareness of future impact.
In line with expectationsA range of elements with valid observations and some relevant self-analysis. Clear judgements with some awareness of future impact.
Yet to meet expectationsA very small range of elements, with limited observation and self-analysis. Unclear judgements with limited awareness of future impact.

Key terms

Short definitions of words you will meet in class and in the exam.

Actuator
An output device that causes movement or action, such as a motor, buzzer or solenoid.
Analogue signal
A signal that can take any value within a range, such as the voltage from a light sensor.
Brief
A short description of the problem to be solved and what the solution must do.
Closed loop system
A control system that uses a sensor to feed information back, so the system can correct itself (a thermostat is an example).
Constraint
A limit on a design, such as cost, time, size, materials or safety rules.
Control system
A set of components that works together to make a device or process behave in a chosen way.
Controlled system
A system where components are used to change its behaviour so that it behaves in a specific way.
Defined function
A clear statement of what a system or part is meant to do.
Digital signal
A signal with only two states, on or off (1 or 0).
Design solution
The idea, model or product that answers the brief.
Efficiency
How much of the energy put into a system comes out as useful work. Wasted energy is often lost as heat.
End user
The person who will actually use the product.
Energy conservation
Using less energy by avoiding waste, for example by switching things off or using insulation.
Ergonomics
Designing products to fit the human body and be comfortable and safe to use.
Evaluate
To judge how well something works, using evidence and giving reasons.
Feedback
Information from the output sent back to the input so a system can adjust.
Flowchart
A diagram that uses standard shapes and arrows to show the steps of a process or program.
Input
Something that goes into a system, such as a button press or a reading from a sensor.
Iterative design
Repeating the cycle of designing, making, testing and improving.
Learning log
A record of your work and thinking as you go. It is used as evidence in the CBAs.
Microcontroller
A small programmable computer on one chip that reads inputs and controls outputs.
Open loop system
A control system with no feedback. It carries out its instructions without checking the result (a basic timer is an example).
Output
What a system produces, such as light, sound or movement.
Process
The part of a system that decides what to do with the inputs.
Prototype
A first model of a design, made to test and improve ideas.
Renewable energy
Energy from sources that are naturally replaced, such as wind, solar and hydro.
Risk assessment
Identifying hazards, deciding how likely and serious the harm could be, and choosing ways to reduce it.
Sensor
An input device that detects something in the environment, such as light, heat or movement.
Simulation
Testing an idea using a model, often on a computer, before building the real thing.
Sustainability
Meeting present needs without damaging the environment or the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
System
A group of parts that work together to do a job.
Transformation
A change of one form of energy into another, such as electrical energy into light.
User needs
What the people using a product require from it, such as comfort, safety or ease of use.
